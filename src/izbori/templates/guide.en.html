Observer guide: what's allowed on election day

A short summary of the rules for observers and journalists. The laws themselves are the authority; article references and links are at the end.

Before election day

  • Campaign silence. No campaigning and no published poll estimates for 48 hours before election day and on election day until the polls close.
  • Observers. Domestic organisations register their observers with RIK at least 7 days before election day, international ones at least 10 days before. Observers wear their accreditation at all times.

Who may be inside a polling station

  • The polling board: its standing members and the members nominated by candidates or lists.
  • Accredited observers.
  • Voters, while they vote.
  • Nobody else. Candidates have no right to stay in the station.

The law treats these as breaches of order: gathering in front of the station, party symbols, keeping unofficial lists of who has voted, and filming without permission.

Opening, 7:00

  • The board shows that the ballot box is empty and seals it.
  • The first voter checks the box and signs a control sheet, which goes into the box. The sheet must be found in the box at the count.

Voting, 7:00–20:00

  • Each voter shows a photo ID. The board checks the right index finger under a UV lamp, then sprays it.
  • One ballot per voter, marked alone behind the screen. Nobody may approach the screen.
  • Only a voter who can't mark the ballot themselves may bring a helper. The minutes record how many voters were helped.
  • Voting outside the station must be requested by 11:00 on election day. Three board members nominated by different contestants take the ballot, and at least one of them must come from the opposition. The minutes record it.
  • Voters already in the queue at 20:00 may still vote.

Counting, in this order

  1. Count the signatures in the voter list: these are the voters who voted.
  2. Count the unused ballots and set them aside.
  3. Check the seal and open the box.
  4. Find the control sheet.
  5. Count the invalid ballots.
  6. Count the valid ballots for each candidate or list.

A ballot is valid when the voter's choice is clear.

The minutes

  • The board fills in the minutes (form RG-2) in several copies. One goes to RIK, one to the local commission and one is posted at the polling station; the others go to the contestants' representatives.
  • A board member's objection must be written into the minutes (section 16.1). That entry is what starts a review.
  • Observers don't sign the minutes. They record what they saw in their own organisation's report.

When something goes wrong

  • A serious error, such as more ballots in the box than voters who signed, sends the materials to the commission. It can correct the minutes, declare that the result can't be determined, or annul the vote at that station, which is then repeated.
  • Objections to a station's or the overall result go to RIK within 72 hours of RIK publishing them. RIK's decisions can be appealed to the Administrative Court.

Crimes against electoral rights

Criminal Code, Articles 154–162:

  • vote buying, where both the person offering and the voter accepting are liable;
  • forcing or threatening a voter;
  • voting twice or in someone else's name;
  • breaking the secrecy of the vote;
  • falsifying results;
  • destroying election documents.

For observers in practice

  • Work in pairs. Document, don't confront. Leave if you feel unsafe; your safety comes first.
  • Note the time of everything unusual.
  • At the count, write down the numbers from the minutes: registered, signatures, unused ballots, ballots in the box, invalid, and each contestant's votes.
  • Check that the minutes are posted at the station.
  • Afterwards, compare your numbers with the minutes RIK publishes and with the station's page on this site. If they differ, report it to your organisation.
